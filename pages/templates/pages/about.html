About

I grew up in war-torn Bosnia and came out of it as a refugee with nothing. My schooling was bad — the kind of bad where the curriculum is whatever a teacher can improvise between air-raid sirens. I stayed confused by education for most of my life. The war got me interested in policy; you pay attention when you've lived through what bad policy does. Clarity came later, when I taught myself statistics, philosophy, logic, and math. My education didn't teach me how to think. I had to do it myself.

That path led me to a Ph.D. in philosophy, a decade of teaching critical thinking at the City University of New York, and published research on reasoning, education, and the ethics of upbringing. Philosophy trained me to take arguments apart down to first principles. It also made me allergic to fluff — and most of what's sold as education is exactly that.

When my daughter reached the age for New York's SHSAT, I did what felt natural: I collected every official test the DOE has published — eight years of sample material, 1,368 questions, more than five thousand answer choices — and ran statistical and linguistic analysis on the entire corpus. I mapped reading complexity by year, classified every question by cognitive task, and tagged every wrong answer by the specific trap it sets. Twenty-eight identified traps in total. Nobody had done this before, at least not publicly. Then I did the same for the Hunter test.

What came out of that work is this platform. Every practice test mirrors the real distribution of skills, difficulty, and traps. Every wrong answer is error-tagged. After each test, parents and tutors see exactly which reasoning mistakes cost points — not "needs improvement in reading comprehension," but the specific trap that made a wrong answer look right, how often it repeats, and what to do about it.

I believe anything can be reverse-engineered to first principles and rebuilt from there. No matter how complex the skill, done this way, anybody can learn it. Most educational frameworks are built for a median student that doesn't exist. They fail everyone else. What I'm building is the opposite: analytics down to the atomic level, so every child learns from exactly where they are.

I built it for my own child first. Then it seemed wrong not to share it with every other family facing the same exams with the same uncertainty. If there's anything I can do to help, reach out.
