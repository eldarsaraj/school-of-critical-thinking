About the School

The School of Critical Thinking exists to teach people, at every age and in every context, how to think well in a world that increasingly does the thinking for them.

Why I built this

I have spent years studying how people think, and how education shapes or fails to shape that capacity. My academic work in philosophy, which started in 2014 right after I got my PhD, led me to a conclusion that is hard to argue with once you see it: formal schooling teaches children what to think, almost never how.

That conclusion became personal when my daughter started school. Watching her move through a curriculum that was rich in content but thin on thinking confirmed everything. If I wanted her to learn how to reason clearly, about causes, about uncertainty, about tradeoffs, I would have to teach her myself. That is what I have been doing for the past several years.

The School of Critical Thinking grew out of that effort. But as I built the curriculum, I kept noticing the same gap in a different place: in the organizations where adults spend their working lives. The same people who never learned to reason clearly as children are now making decisions under pressure, evaluating AI-generated output, and navigating uncertainty at real stakes. The problem doesn't go away when school ends. It just gets more expensive.

So the School now serves both. A curriculum for children, ages 8 to 16, built from the ground up. And talks, workshops, and training programs for organizations that recognize critical thinking as the most undervalued professional skill they have. The same intellectual foundation, applied at every stage of life.

Eldar Sarajlic
Founder, The School of Critical Thinking

One idea

All the work here is organized around a single governing idea: critical thinking as resistance to entropy.

The world tends toward disorder. Information arrives unstructured. Confident-sounding nonsense spreads faster than careful argument. AI systems generate plausible text at near-zero cost, accelerating everything. Thinking critically is the act of pushing back: making sense of what is chaotic, naming what is unnamed, modeling what is opaque.

Entropy is not used here as merely decorative language. It is the conceptual engine of the entire curriculum, present in the children's books, in the adult work, and in every corporate program. It gives the School a single coherent identity across audiences.

The framework

The curriculum is built around five structures: the patterns that govern how the world actually behaves. These are taught from first principles. The same five foundations operate at every age; the examples, the register, and the stakes change, but the underlying skills do not.

  • Patterns: recognizing structure in the world, and knowing when an apparent pattern is noise
  • Causality: understanding what produces what, not just what follows what
  • Uncertainty: reasoning clearly when you can't know the outcome in advance
  • Models: how every explanation simplifies reality, and how to use that usefully
  • Decisions: incentives, tradeoffs, and how to make choices that hold up under pressure

A nine-year-old learning to spot a false pattern and a leadership team learning to decompose a complex strategic problem are doing the same thing at different scales. That is what makes this a school, not a collection of separate products.

Who we serve

The School serves three distinct groups, each with its own offer and register, but all on the same intellectual foundation.

Families. Parents who want to give their children the thinking skills schools rarely teach directly. A three-level book series spanning ages 8 to 16, with 52 lessons per module, designed for parent-led weekly instruction. No specialist background required.

Learners. Students preparing for high-stakes exams like the SHSAT, children learning analytical thinking through Python Detective, and adults who want to understand where their own reasoning breaks down. The free Thinking Diagnostic is the entry point for the adult learner.

Organizations. Companies, leadership teams, and L&D departments that recognize critical thinking as the most undervalued skill in their workforce. Talks, half-day workshops, and multi-session programs built on the same foundations as the books and curriculum.
