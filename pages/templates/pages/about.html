About the School

There is a gap in education that almost nobody talks about. Schools teach children facts, skills, and subject knowledge. History dates, grammar rules, mathematical formulas. These things matter. But almost no school ever teaches children how to actually think: how to spot a real pattern, how to distinguish a cause from a coincidence, how to reason when the information is incomplete, how to make a decision and stand behind it. These skills are never on the syllabus, and most people reach adulthood without them.

I have been studying this gap for most of my professional life. My academic work in philosophy, which started in 2014 after I got my PhD, kept returning to the same conclusion: formal education is almost entirely about content, and almost never about the cognitive skills that make content useful.

The conclusion became personal when my daughter started school. Watching her move through a curriculum that was rich in facts and thin on thinking confirmed everything. If I wanted her to learn how to reason clearly, about causes, about uncertainty, about tradeoffs, I would have to teach her myself. That is what I have been doing.

But as I built that curriculum, I kept noticing the same gap somewhere else: in the organizations where adults spend their working lives. The same people who never learned to reason clearly as children are now making decisions under pressure, evaluating AI-generated output, and navigating uncertainty at real stakes. The problem does not go away when school ends. It just gets more expensive.

The School of Critical Thinking exists to address both. A structured curriculum for children aged 8 to 16, built from first principles. Talks and workshops for organizations that take critical thinking seriously as a professional competency. Tools for individual learners who want to understand where their own reasoning breaks down.

All of it is organized around one idea: that the world tends toward disorder. Information arrives unstructured. Confident-sounding nonsense spreads faster than careful argument. AI systems generate plausible text at near-zero cost, accelerating everything. Thinking critically is the act of pushing back: bringing order to chaos through clear reasoning. I call this resisting entropy. It is the conceptual engine of everything here, from the children's books to the executive workshops.

The curriculum is built around five foundations that operate at every age:

  • Patterns: recognizing structure in the world, and knowing when an apparent pattern is noise
  • Causality: understanding what produces what, not just what follows what
  • Uncertainty: reasoning clearly when you cannot know the outcome in advance
  • Models: how every explanation simplifies reality, and how to use that usefully
  • Decisions: incentives, tradeoffs, and how to make choices that hold up under pressure

A nine-year-old learning to spot a false pattern and a leadership team learning to decompose a complex strategic problem are doing the same thing at different scales. That is what makes this a school.
