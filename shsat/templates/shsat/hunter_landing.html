The Rule That Never Says No

A horoscope never tells you something that couldn't be true. "You value loyalty but have been let down." "A challenge at work will test your patience." These are not predictions. They are descriptions so broad that almost anyone, reading almost any one of them, will find a fit — not because the stars are accurate, but because the statement was never specific enough to be wrong.

Psychologists call this the Barnum effect, after the showman who understood that a vague positive statement will be claimed by nearly every listener as personally meaningful. The key is not that people are gullible. It is that the statement has been engineered to be unfalsifiable: there is no possible life to which "you sometimes doubt yourself" does not apply.

The trouble is not confined to astrology columns. The same structure — a claim shaped so that no evidence could ever count against it — appears in advertising, in political promises, and in the kind of advice that sounds wise precisely because it commits to nothing. The rule that never says no is not a rule at all. It is a sentence dressed up as one.
