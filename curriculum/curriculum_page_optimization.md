# Curriculum Page Optimization Brief
## School of Critical Thinking — Seeing Patterns (Module 1)

This document identifies what to add, change, or clarify on the curriculum pages to make them more intuitive for parents evaluating the curriculum and reviewers like Cathy Duffy assessing it formally. Every suggestion preserves your voice, your positioning, and your approach. Nothing here changes what the curriculum is — only how clearly it communicates what it already is.

---

## 1. Add an "At a Glance" block to the Seeing Patterns module page

This is the single highest-impact change. Reviewers and busy parents scan before they read. A compact reference block near the top of the page answers the practical questions immediately, before the reader has to hunt for them. Place it directly below the opening description, before the "What's inside" section.

**Add a block containing:**

- **Age range:** 8–11
- **Duration:** 52 lessons · one per week · one full school year
- **Lesson length:** 20–30 minutes per lesson
- **Format:** Physical book (paperback) and ebook
- **Who teaches it:** Parent-led · no specialist background required
- **Can a child use it independently?** Lessons are written to be read aloud or by the child, but the exercises and discussion questions are designed for parent-child dialogue
- **Religious viewpoint:** Secular · no religious content or perspective
- **Intended use:** Enrichment supplement · works alongside any core curriculum
- **Grade equivalent:** Roughly grades 3–5, but skill-based rather than grade-locked

The religious viewpoint and "enrichment supplement" lines are the two most critical additions. They answer the two questions every homeschool parent and every formal reviewer asks first, and your page currently answers neither.

---

## 2. Clarify the parent's role more explicitly

The current page says "no specialist background required" and "full lesson plans are included" — both good. But it doesn't explain *what a parent actually does* in a typical lesson. Parents evaluating a curriculum want to know: how much preparation does this take? Do I read it with my child? Do I just hand them the book?

**Add a short "How you teach it" section** (3–4 sentences) that describes the typical lesson flow:

Each lesson opens with a short story or real-world example that the parent reads aloud or the child reads independently. A thinking question follows, designed to spark a conversation — not to be answered in a workbook. Exercises and discussion questions complete each lesson; these are where the parent's role matters most. Total preparation time per lesson is minimal: the lesson plan is built into the book.

This mirrors language already on your About page ("designed to be taught by parents, with discussion and exercises that make the material come alive") but makes it concrete and practical at the point of decision.

---

## 3. Strengthen the opening description of the module

The current opening — *"The world contains patterns — but not every pattern is real"* — is good. It's sharp and conceptually honest. But it works better as a tagline than as an opening description, because it leads with the critical/skeptical dimension before establishing the positive case.

A reviewer trying to categorize the curriculum and a parent deciding whether to buy it both need to understand the full arc of what the book does before they encounter the caveat about false patterns.

**Consider restructuring the opening paragraph:**

Lead with what children will gain (the positive case), then introduce the critical dimension as what makes it serious rather than what makes it skeptical. The current Part 4 description — *"The most important section of the book. The brain is so good at finding patterns that it regularly invents ones that aren't there"* — is excellent and can inform the revision. The overall arc (discover → recognise → find hidden → detect false → use actively) should be legible from the opening paragraph alone.

---

## 4. Add one sentence explicitly positioning it as a supplement

The curriculum page and module page never use the word "supplement." Parents who are already using a core curriculum (math, language arts, history, science) need to know immediately that this doesn't replace any of those — it adds a dimension none of them cover. The About page says "fit into your existing educational efforts" but that phrase doesn't appear on the curriculum or module pages.

**Add one explicit line** near the top of both the curriculum overview page and the Seeing Patterns module page:

*"Designed as a weekly supplement — one lesson fits alongside any existing homeschool or after-school curriculum without displacing it."*

This is also exactly what differentiates you from a full-day or core curriculum program, which is important for Cathy Duffy's categorization.

---

## 5. Make the five-part structure easier to scan on the module page

The five parts are currently described in full paragraphs, which is good for depth but harder to scan quickly. The curriculum overview page already uses bullet points well for each module — apply the same logic inside the module page itself.

**Consider adding a compact arc summary** between the opening description and the five-part breakdown:

| Part | Focus | Lessons |
|------|-------|---------|
| I — Discovering Patterns | Noticing hidden structure in the world | 1–10 |
| II — Recognising Patterns | Classification, sequences, cycles, prediction | 11–20 |
| III — Hidden Patterns | Patterns in games, language, behavior, markets | 21–30 |
| IV — When Patterns Mislead | Coincidence, bias, superstition, false patterns | 31–40 |
| V — Using Patterns to Think | Applying patterns as active thinking tools | 41–52 |

This gives a reviewer or parent the full shape of the book in ten seconds, and makes the logical progression (from discovery to application, through the critical middle section) immediately visible.

---

## 6. Add a short "Who this is for" section to the module page

The About page and the curriculum overview both address who the curriculum is for. The module page — the one a parent lands on when they're closest to purchasing — doesn't. A parent who arrives directly at the Seeing Patterns page via a Google search or a curriculum review link may never see the About page.

**Add 3–4 sentences:**

This module is designed for children aged 8–11, whether they are homeschooled full-time or studying at home after school. It works as a standalone course or as part of the full nine-module curriculum. Parents with no background in philosophy, logic, or education can teach it directly from the book. It is equally suited to children who are advanced readers and those who prefer to work through lessons with a parent.

---

## 7. Surface the discussion-based nature more prominently

The curriculum is explicitly not a workbook — it's built around stories, thinking questions, and conversation. This is one of its strongest differentiating features and is mentioned on the About page but not on the module page. Many parents are actively looking for discussion-based alternatives to fill-in-the-blank workbooks, and parents who find workbooks deadening will respond strongly to this distinction.

**Add a brief line** in the "How it works" section:

*"Lessons are built around stories and discussion questions, not worksheets. The exercises require thinking and conversation — not fill-in-the-blank answers."*

---

## 8. Minor language consistency issue

The curriculum overview page uses British spelling ("behaviour") while the module page uses it inconsistently. The About page uses American spelling in most places. Since you're publishing on KDP and targeting a US homeschool market primarily, settle on American spelling throughout the website (behavior, color, etc.) for consistency. This is a small point but reviewers notice it.

---

## What not to change

- The voice and tone throughout the site is distinctive, confident, and clear. Do not make it warmer, softer, or more conventionally marketing-like. The directness is a feature.
- The philosophical framing ("five structures that govern reality") is unusual and precisely right — it's what makes the curriculum different from logic puzzles and fallacy lists. Do not water it down.
- The "no specialist background required" message is already well-placed and should stay exactly as it is.
- The sample lesson CTA is well-executed. No changes needed there.
